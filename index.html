AI-assisted astronomy

Tell it what to shoot. The AI runs the night.

These are real messages from field nights at a 2,800 mm telescope in a Bortle 8 backyard. The owner typed a few words on a phone. An AI ran Alan Astro and did the rest: the profile, polar alignment, focus, guiding, the plan and the flats.

  1. 1
    Install the server

    Put Alan Astro on the computer at your telescope: a Windows PC, a Linux mini PC or a Raspberry Pi 4 or 5. That's your astro server.

  2. 2
    Tell the AI its name

    Give the AI your astro server's name, like astro-pc. The server describes its own API, so the AI learns what it can do from the server itself. Coming with the release.

  3. 3
    Say what to shoot

    A target and the sky you can see. The AI runs the night and tells you how it went.

Guiding

Round stars, even at 2,800 mm.

At 2,800 mm, one arcsecond of error covers several pixels. A guider that only thinks in pixels needs new settings for every scope. Alan Astro's guider checks its limits in arcseconds of sky, so a setting that suits a 250 mm refractor can't end up too small on a 2,800 mm SCT.

  • Limits that know your scope

    The minimum move, the settle limit and the search area each have a floor in arcseconds, worked out for your camera and focal length, so none of them can be set too small.

  • Knows your guide setup

    A guide scope, an off-axis guider or a camera's built-in guide sensor, with binning counted in. It won't start guiding until it knows the pixel scale.

  • Several stars at once

    It follows up to five stars and uses the middle value of their movement, so one star in bad seeing can't pull the mount off.

  • Picks clean stars

    It chooses stars that stand out clearly from the background, not just the brightest, and can skip the stretched stars at the edge of an off-axis guider. On one field, that alone took guiding from 3.38″ to 1.29″ RMS.

  • A guiding assistant

    It watches the mount drift with no corrections, tells seeing apart from real drift, and suggests a minimum move.

Dusk to dawn

Plan it in the evening. Wake up to data.

A plan runs the whole night, from cooling the camera at dusk to parking the mount at dawn. If the weather turns bad, it pauses and carries on when the sky clears.

Also included

Everything else a night needs.

Autofocus that shows its work

It steps the focuser through focus, fits a curve through the star sizes and tells you how well the curve fits. You don't have to measure backlash first: every move finishes from the same side, so slack in the gears can't move the result. It applies the focus offset you set for each filter, and if a run fails, it says so.

0.12′

Plate solving that catches mistakes

It solves on your own computer, with no internet. On a real night it centred a target to 0.12 arcminutes in two tries. It also reports the focal length it measured, so you'll notice if you forgot the reducer.

  • Works offline
  • Centres as close as you ask
  • Names what's in the frame
  • Measured vs. expected focal length

Polar alignment

Three measurements, then it shows which way to turn the altitude and azimuth knobs, and how far.

Live stacking

Frames are lined up on their stars and stacked as they arrive, with your darks and flats applied. Bad frames are left out, and it tells you why. It can stack a running plan as it goes.

Star analysis

Star size (HFR), star count and roundness for every frame, plus a tilt map that shows which way stars stretch across the field.

A sky atlas for tonight

Over 18,900 objects: OpenNGC, the Messier, Caldwell and Abell lists, nebula and cluster catalogues such as Sharpless and Barnard, and the planets and comets. It shows what gets high enough in darkness tonight, in the directions you can see. Each target has its altitude curve with your horizon drawn on it, when it's highest and how far it is from the Moon. There's a mosaic planner too.

Safe while you sleep

Six weather limits (wind, gusts, rain, cloud, humidity and dew) pause the night and pick it up again when conditions clear, instead of ending it. A safety monitor that stops answering counts as unsafe. Your own horizon applies to every slew.
